## Coach-style assessment

The athlete is explosive and coordinated enough to turn a high outdoor set into a forceful attack. His strongest features are the arm swing into takeoff, vertical intent, torso rotation, and balanced two-foot landing. The main performance ceiling is not effort. It is repeatable spacing and sequencing. A curved, bounding approach brings him almost under the set, then both elbows fold early in the air. That leaves a compact late swing and a contact point close to the head. The rep can still be effective, but it gives away reach, vision, and late shot options. Because the clip is slowed, actual approach speed and real-time tempo should not be scored from playback cadence.

## Highest-value corrections

1. **Create more space under the ball.** Begin the final acceleration a fraction farther back and finish the plant behind the projected ball path. The target is contact above and slightly in front of the right shoulder, not directly over the head. Use an approach-and-catch drill: three sets of six, freeze at full reach, then repeat with controlled roll shots.
2. **Make the final two steps clearer.** Keep the build slow-to-fast, make the right penultimate step long and low, and snap the left close step down quickly. Avoid turning the penultimate into a bound. Use four sets of five unloaded right-left plants, then three sets of five with a toss.
3. **Lengthen the aerial sequence.** Reach the left hand toward the ball while keeping the right elbow high and back, then pull the left elbow toward the ribs as the right arm accelerates. Tennis-ball throws over the net, three sets of eight, can train the trunk-to-shoulder-to-arm sequence without chasing maximum force.
4. **Preserve the landing.** Keep the current two-foot absorption and make the landing base roughly shoulder width when the set allows. This is already the most complete phase in the rep.

## Tactical interpretation

The goal is late selection from one repeatable load, not a different visible wind-up for each target.
